ABSTRACT
Background: Accurate classification of electroencephalography (EEG) signals depends on the optimal combina -
tion of signal processing, feature extraction, and classification methods. Since no single approach is suitable 
across different domains, identifying the best methods for each application remains a critical challenge.
Objective: We propose EEGOpt, a Bayesian optimization framework designed to automate and optimize meth -
odological choices in electroencephalography (EEG) signal processing and classification.
Methods: EEGOpt employed Tree-Structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) to optimize signal denoising, feature 
extraction, and classifier selection. The search space included Empirical Mode Decomposition and Wavelet 
Packet Decomposition (WPD) for denoising; spatiotemporal, nonlinear, and spectral features; and classifiers with 
distinct decision boundaries. A modular caching mechanism was used to minimize redundant computations. 
EEGOpt was evaluated on three datasets and benchmarked against deep-learning models (EEGNet, Shallow -
ConvNet, and DeepConvNet). TPE was compared with sampling methods, including Gaussian Process, Covari -
ance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy, Quasi-Monte Carlo, and random search.
Results: EEGOpt achieved classification accuracies of up to 99.63 %, outperforming EEGNet (96.20 %), Shal -
lowConvNet (90.83 %), and DeepConvNet (90.29 %). The caching mechanism reduced computation time by 
74.69 % compared to no caching, and by 95 % compared to deep learning models. TPE was effective in navi -
gating hierarchical search spaces to locate global optima. EEGOpt identified covariance and wavelet features, k- 
nearest neighbor classifier, and WPD denoising as optimal for music-based EEG classification.
Conclusion: EEGOpt is a scalable and interpretable framework that automatically identifies optimal signal pro -
cessing and classification strategies adaptable to EEG datasets, making it a valuable tool for neuroscientific 
research, diagnostics, and brain-computer interface development.
1. Introduction
The integration of Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) 
methods with brain signal analysis has transformed human-machine 
interaction [ 1 , 2 ], with applications ranging from mental-state and 
emotion recognition to assistive technologies, cognitive assessment, and 
more [ 3 – 7 ]. This growing field focuses on applying computational 
models to decode brain activity using electroencephalography (EEG) 
signals. EEG is preferred for its noninvasive nature, cost-efficiency, and 
potential for developing wearable devices [ 8 – 10 ].
Automated classification of EEG signals is a prominent research di -
rection in EEG signal analysis, mainly using convolutional neural net -
works (ConvNets) [ 11 ]. For example, EEGNet has been used to classify 
visual, error-related negativity, and sensory-motor rhythms [ 12 ]. 
Schirrmeister et al. evaluated deep and shallow ConvNets [ 13 ], and 
Alizadeh et al. employed LeNet and AlexNet on motor imagery datasets 
[ 14 ]. Automated depression screening was performed using ConvNets 
[ 15 ] and InceptionTime models [ 16 ]. DL models, such as ResNet and 
InceptionNet, have achieved promising results for seizure detection 
[ 17 ]. Although deep learners alleviate the need for manual feature 
extraction, these methods require large amounts of training data, pro -
vide minimal information regarding the underlying neural processes, 
and can be of limited use in cases where computational efficiency is 
essential [ 3 , 18 ]. Furthermore, DL approaches often require manual data 
preprocessing and signal cleaning, thereby diminishing their appeal as 
automated solutions [ 11 , 13 , 19 ].

Researchers have shown that dynamic feature extraction methods can be 
used to automate ML-based EEG classification [ 18 ]. For example, Tasci 
et al. proposed self-organized EEG classification models based on 
quantum-inspired local binary patterns for dynamic feature extraction 
in psychiatric disorder detection [ 20 ]. Similarly, dynamic feature

been used for the automated detection of dynamical changes in EEG [ 21 ,
22 ]. Recent Reimann geometry-based approaches allow advanced 
feature extraction (such as spatial covariance matrices and 
manifold-based transformations) without the need for extensive manual 
selection of features, with applications in the automated detection of 
cognitive load [ 23 ], epilepsy [ 24 ] and motor imagery [ 25 ]. However, 
even with such methods, the need for manual signal preprocessing, 
classifier selection, and model tuning remains.
A typical EEG classification pipeline consists of three major steps: 
signal preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification. Each step 
offers a wide array of methodological options, and determining the 
optimal combination of these methods is challenging [ 26 , 27 ]. The effi -
cacy of the pipeline is influenced by multiple factors, including the 
quality of EEG data acquisition, selection of preprocessing and feature 
extraction methods, choice of classification algorithm, and tuning of 
model hyperparameters [ 28 , 29 ]. Studies have shown that the perfor -
mance of a chosen classifier depends on the choice of the feature 
extraction method, and no single classifier or feature extraction method 
is optimal across different domains [ 30 – 32 ]. Moreover, there is a 
notable gap in understanding how various EEG preprocessing steps 
impact performance [ 33 – 35 ]. For example, signal denoising signifi -
cantly affects the efficacy of feature extraction and classification 
[ 36 – 38 ], and the efficacy of denoising methods varies depending on the 
dataset [ 39 – 41 ]. Each decision within this complex pipeline critically 
influences the overall effectiveness of the system [ 31 , 42 ]. As noted by 
Lotte et al., researchers often use trial-and-error methods or domain 
knowledge to select the optimal configurations for their application 
domains. However, it is difficult to ascertain whether the applied 
methodology is indeed optimal and there is a need to evaluate different 
methods to determine suitable techniques [ 43 ]. This can be solved by 
designing a framework that can search for the optimal configuration of 
the EEG pipeline while maintaining a minimal computational cost.
The necessity for such a framework was also highlighted in our 
previous work on the neural dynamics of music perception [ 44 ]. The 
study focused on identifying the neural dynamics underlying the 
perception of Indian classical music (ICM) ragas through a statistically 
rigorous comparison of multiple EEG feature and classifier combina -
tions. While the domain-specific approach was effective for the targeted 
stimulus and successfully demonstrated the efficacy of multimodal 
feature combinations, it also emphasized the need to capture and opti -
mize interdependencies between EEG signal processing pipeline stages 
and address generalizability across datasets. The extensive comparisons 
required for such an analysis highlighted the need for a systematic and 
scalable framework capable of automating the search for optimal EEG 
processing pipelines.
To address these challenges, we propose a novel framework called 
EEGOpt to automate the selection of optimal EEG signal classification 
pipeline. The selection of optimal methods for EEG classification is

classification performance. Considering a hyperparameter space Θ , 
representing the set of all potential hyperparameter configurations of a 
classification pipeline for a particular EEG task, the search for optimal

BO has primarily been used for classifier tuning in EEG signal clas -

Kahmi et al. applied BO to optimize ConvNet ensemble for EEG-based

Yadav et al. employed BO to optimize a double Long Short-Term

to optimize filter configurations in motor imagery classification studies

windows length, EEG channels, and frequency bands for feature 
extraction [ 64 ]. However, to the best of our knowledge, BO or other 
optimization algorithms have not been previously applied to select 
optimal methods across all stages of EEG classification, ranging from 
signal preprocessing to classifier selection and model tuning. Our study 
addresses this gap by using BO to optimize hyperparameters across the 
entire EEG classification pipeline.
EEGOpt is an automated EEG classification framework that de -
termines optimal methodologies and hyperparameters for EEG signal 
denoising, feature extraction, dimensionality reduction, and classifier 
selection, eliminating the need for manual trial-and-error selection. It 
uses Tree-structured Parzen Estimators (TPE), a BO method, to deter -
mine the optimal configurations across the entire EEG processing pipe -
line. A hyperparameter space containing different approaches used in 
EEG signal classification is defined, and the framework iteratively 
evaluates different combinations to identify those best suited to the 
input dataset. The EEG classification pipeline inherently forms a com -
plex hierarchical search space, with interdependent decisions at multi -
ple stages (preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification). 
Although TPE is particularly effective in navigating such non-uniform, 
conditional, categorical, and high-dimensional spaces, we also bench -
marked its performance against four widely used hyperparameter opti -
mization methods: Gaussian Process (GP), Covariance Matrix 
Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES), Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) 
sampling, and Random Search (RS).
Unlike DL-based EEG classification models that provide minimal 
information regarding underlying neural processes, EEGOpt allows us to 
understand the interaction between various methodological choices in 
the EEG classification pipeline, thereby decoding the underlying neural 
dynamics. However, because EEGOpt is a sequential search framework, 
it involves repeated iterations of signal processing steps, leading to high 
computational costs, which can be practically intractable for long search 
processes. To address this challenge, EEGOpt incorporates a modular 
cache management system that stores intermediate signal trans -
formations as cache files to minimize redundant computations. We 
evaluated EEGOpt on three music-based EEG datasets and compared its 
performance with optimized DL models, including EEGNet, Shallow -
ConvNet, and DeepConvNet. The results demonstrate that EEGOpt 
outperforms DL models in both classification accuracy and 
computational efficiency while offering greater interpretability and 
flexibility.
2. Material and methods
2.1. EEGOpt framework
EEGOpt utilizes BO to identify the optimal choices across denoising, 
feature extraction, dimensionality reduction, and classification stages. 
The EEGOpt framework is shown in a block diagram ( Fig. 1 ) and as 
pseudocode ( Algorithm 1 ). EEG data from multiple participants in a 
dataset, labeled according to the experimental phases, were input into 
the processing pipeline. The framework initiates hyperparameter com -
binations to select the denoising method, feature set, PCA components, 
and classification algorithm. This forms the hyperparameter space, as 
shown in dotted lines in Fig. 1 . BO using TPE was used for hyper -
parameter search. In each trial, TPE proposes a combination of hyper -
parameters spanning all pipeline stages.
The denoising stage includes three options: no denoising, wavelet 
packet decomposition (WPD), and empirical mode decomposition 
(EMD). WPD and EMD were selected due to their robustness in EEG 
signal denoising, and the degree of denoising can be controlled via 
minimal hyperparameters. Post signal denoising, a sliding window 
approach was used for feature extraction. The feature extraction options 
included covariance (Cov), wavelet (Wave), fractal (Frac), entropy 
(Ent), and statistical (Stat) features. These were selected to capture 
spatial-temporal coordination, spectral, nonlinear, and temporal 
dynamics.
EEG data from each participant was individually processed through 
the denoising and feature extraction pipeline. The feature vectors from 
all participants were concatenated to form a single feature matrix. 
During each optimization trial, this matrix is randomly divided into 
training (80 %) and test (20 %) sets using a fixed random seed for 
reproducibility. The training set was exclusively used for model training 
and tuning, while the test set remained completely unseen during 
optimization and was used solely for final model evaluation. The high 
dimensionality of the resulting feature set was reduced using principal 
component analysis (PCA). PCA was chosen for its simplicity, and ability 
to preserve essential information while reducing dimensionality.
In the classification stage, five classification algorithms were 
considered: KNN, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression 
(LR), Random Forest (RF), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). These 
classifiers represent a broad range of decision boundaries commonly 
used in EEG classification. Individual hyperparameters were optimized 
for each algorithm. Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) averaged 
over 5-fold cross-validation on the training set was used to evaluate 
classification performance, which served as the objective function for 
the search process. TPE used MCC scores from previous trials to guide 
the selection of promising hyperparameter combinations in subsequent 
trials, progressively converging toward optimal configurations. A 
caching mechanism was developed to store intermediate signal trans -
formations as cache files, thereby preventing redundant computations. 
A pruning strategy was used to terminate underperforming trials early, 
reducing computational overhead. Upon optimization completion, the 
best hyperparameter configuration was used to reprocess the dataset 
and train the classifier model on the training set, which was then eval -
uated on the held-out test set to assess generalization performance. The 
entire process was repeated with different random seeds to ensure result

2.2. Dataset description
EEGOpt was evaluated using three publicly available EEG datasets.
2.2.1. Indian classical music EEG dataset (ICM)
The ICM dataset contains EEG recordings from nine participants who 
listened to three ICM ragas: Ahir Bhairav, Bhimpalashri and Yaman 
Kalyan, performed by Pandit Ravi Shankar. Each participant listened to 
2-min instrumental recordings of the three ragas. Each raga was 
associated with a distinct percept and was played twice in a randomized 
order, with a 30-s rest between playbacks, forming a four-class classi -
fication task (three music phases and one rest phase). The entire session 
lasted 17 min per participant: 4 min per music condition and 5 min of 
total rest. EEG signals were recorded using a 24-channel RMS Maximus® 
24 system (India Pvt. Ltd.) with 19 Ag-AgCl sintered ring electrodes 
placed on the scalp, following the international 10–20 system. Signals 
were sampled at 256 Hz using a referential montage with the reference 
electrode placed at the nasion. The data were subsequently re- 
referenced using common average referencing. A detailed description 
of the dataset is given in Ref. [ 44 ].
2.2.2. Naturalistic music EEG dataset – Elgar (NMED-E)
The NMED-E is a publicly available dataset containing EEG re -
cordings from 24 participants who listened to the first movement of 
Elgar’s Cello Concerto in E Minor. A phase-scrambled version of the 
original music served as the control stimulus. Both stimuli lasted 480 s 
(8 min), forming a binary classification task. Data were recorded using a 
128-channel Electrical Geodesics Inc. (EGI) GES 300 system, sampled at 
1 kHz and later downsampled to 125 Hz. The EEG was referenced to Cz 
during acquisition and subsequently re-referenced using common 
average referencing. A detailed description of the dataset and signal 
preprocessing steps is given in Ref. [ 65 ].
2.2.3. Naturalistic music EEG dataset – Minimalism (NMED-M)
The NMED-M is a publicly available dataset consisting of EEG re -
cordings from 30 participants who listened to five stimuli based on Steve 
Reich’s Piano Phase. These stimuli included the original piece, a remix 
version, and three variations: Abrupt Change, Segment Shuffle, and 
Tremolo. This dataset was used for a five-class classification task, with 
each stimulus lasting for 5 min. Similar to the NMED-E dataset, data 
were recorded using the same 128-channel EGI GES 300 system, with 
recordings sampled at 1 kHz, and later downsampled to 125 Hz, and 
referenced to Cz and later re-referenced using common average refer -
encing. A detailed description of the dataset and signal preprocessing 
steps is given in Ref. [ 66 ]. For the NMED-E and NMED-M datasets, 
preprocessed EEG data provided by the dataset authors were used as the 
starting point for automated denoising, feature extraction and classifi -
cation using EEGOpt.
All computations were conducted using Python on a cloud-based 
system running Ubuntu 20.04.6 LTS, equipped with 32 Intel Xeon 
Platinum 8470 vCPUs, 64 GB RAM, and 200 GB storage.
2.3. Signal denoising
EEG signals are inherently nonlinear and nonstationary, comprising 
both transient events and sustained rhythms, and are often corrupted by 
diverse artifacts. Effective denoising is therefore essential to preserve the 
integrity of neural information for subsequent analysis. The choice of 
denoising method for the classification pipeline was based on three key 
criteria. First, the method should enable selective suppression of noise 
across frequency bands while preserving key neural components. Sec -
ond, the denoising process can be controlled using minimal hyper -
parameters. Third, the method should have proven efficacy in previous 
studies.
Traditional approaches exhibit important limitations in this context. 
Fixed-bandwidth filters risk attenuating neural activity that overlap 
with noise frequencies [ 67 ]. Independent Component Analysis requires 
high-dimensional data and manual component selection, making it less 
suited for automated pipelines [ 68 , 69 ]. Adaptive filtering depends on 
accurate noise modeling and requires reference noise channels. Simi -
larly, Wiener filtering assumes signal stationarity, a condition violated 
in EEG due to transient neural events [ 68 ].
To overcome these limitations, two data-driven, adaptive denoising 
methods were selected: WPD and EMD. Compared to discrete wavelet 
transforms, WPD provides higher frequency resolution via complete

making it suitable for capturing nonlinear and aperiodic signal struc -

isolating transient artifacts in early IMFs while preserving slower 
rhythms in later modes [ 73 ]. It has shown strong performance in studies 
on seizure detection, cognitive tasks, and emotional state recognition 
[ 73 – 75 ]. Both WPD and EMD meet our selection criteria: they do not 
assume stationarity, resolve spectral overlaps, require minimal hyper -
parameters for seamless integration into EEGOpt, and are extensively 
validated in EEG literature.
2.3.1. Wavelet packet decomposition
WPD is an extension of the wavelet transform that decomposes a 
signal into a tree-like structure, where each node represents a different 
frequency sub-band. This hierarchical partitioning maintains uniform 
frequency resolution across the EEG spectrum. Each node in the wavelet 
packet tree contains coefficients that describe the amplitude or strength 
of the corresponding frequency components in the signal. To denoise the 
original signal, thresholding was applied to the coefficients of each sub- 
band, effectively reducing noise while preserving the relevant signal 
features.
To optimize the WPD denoising process, a custom implementation 
was developed that allowed the degree of denoising to be controlled by a 
single tunable hyperparameter: the percentage threshold. The steps are 
as follows. 
i. The signal is decomposed to a predefined level, and the wavelet 
coefficients are extracted.

Therefore, the WPD method involves two key settings: the choice of 
mother wavelet and the percentage threshold level. Unlike conventional 
node-based thresholding, which removes an entire frequency band, this 
approach thresholds the coefficients within each node. This results in a 
more granular control over noise suppression, allowing for the removal 
of noise without eliminating potentially valuable neural activity present 
in the same sub-band. As a result of the more localized coefficient-level 
thresholding, higher threshold values are typically necessary to observe 
visible differences in EEG morphology and Power Spectral Density 
(PSD), as shown in Fig. 2 . WPD was performed using the PyWavelets 
library [ 76 ].
2.3.2. Empirical mode decomposition
EMD decomposes a signal into a set of IMFs, each capturing oscil -
latory modes at distinct time scales. An IMF satisfies two conditions: the 
number of extrema and zero crossings must either be equal or differ by at 
most one, and the mean value of the envelopes defined by the local 
maxima and minima must be zero at any point. This data-driven sifting 
adapts to local signal properties, making it ideal for capturing nonsta -
tionary artifacts. Energy-based thresholding is then applied to denoise 
the signal. The process involves the following steps. 
i. The original signal is divided into segments as EMD is computa -
tionally intensive.
ii. The segments are decomposed into a series of IMFs through a 
process called sifting.
iii. An energy threshold is applied, and IMFs with energy below the 
threshold are discarded.
iv. The denoised segments are reconstructed by summing the 
selected IMFs, and the segments are concatenated to obtain the 
denoised EEG signal.

%. EMD was performed using the PyEMD library [ 77 ].
2.4. Feature extraction
The selection of feature extraction methods in this study encom -
passed statistical, covariance, wavelet-based spectral, fractal, and en -
tropy features. The feature sets were chosen to capture distinct and 
complementary aspects of EEG signals relevant to classification, that is, 
linear, nonlinear, and spatiotemporal neural dynamics. Statistical fea -
tures capture time-domain properties such as amplitude variability, 
whereas covariance features reflect spatial coordination across channels 
[ 78 ]. Wavelet-based features capture frequency-specific neural oscilla -
tions [ 26 ]. Entropy and fractal features quantify nonlinear signal 
complexity and irregularity [ 70 ]. These features represent important 
facets of neural dynamics and have demonstrated efficacy across diverse 
EEG classification tasks.
Feature extraction was performed using a sliding window of 1 s with 
50 % overlap. Features from the lagging window were added to each 
feature vector to capture temporal dependencies. The extraction of 
statistical and covariance features followed the methods described in 
previous research papers [ 78 – 80 ]. Statistical features included the EEG 
signal ’ s first-, second-, third-, and fourth-order moments extracted from 
each time window. The maximum and minimum values and paired 
differences in the first-order moments across the half windows were also 
calculated. Covariance features included the covariance matrix of the 
EEG channels, the logarithm of the covariance matrix, and the

2.5. Principal component analysis
PCA is a dimensionality reduction technique commonly used in EEG 
signal processing [ 82 ]. In this study, PCA was applied to reduce the 
dimensionality of the extracted feature set prior to classification. The 
number of PCA components plays a crucial role in balancing computa -
tional efficiency with the preservation of signal variance. To address 
this, the cumulative explained variance was used as a tunable hyper -
parameter to determine the optimal number of components. In addition, 
PCA was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the NMED-E and 
NMED-M datasets from 125 EEG channels to 19 channels, matching the 
channel count of the ICM dataset. This significantly reduced the 
computational burden of the subsequent signal processing steps.
2.6. Classification
The classifiers evaluated in this study were SVM, RF, KNN, LR, and 
MLP. These models were chosen to cover a broad range of decision 
boundaries and learning paradigms commonly used in EEG classifica -
tion tasks. LR uses a linear decision boundary whereas SVM can model 
both linear and nonlinear relationships by maximizing the separating 
hyperplane between classes. It is also the most widely used classifier in 
EEG studies [ 26 , 83 ]. KNN is a non-parametric, distance-based method 
that adapts well to complex and irregular feature distributions and has 
been widely used in emotion recognition and mental state classification 
[ 83 ]. An MLP is a neural network capable of learning complex decision 
boundaries. RF is a tree-based ensemble that uses the bagging of mul -
tiple weak decision trees. These classifiers were selected for their algo -
rithmic diversity and established efficacy across EEG applications [ 3 , 26 ,
30 , 83 ]. Their inclusion ensured that EEGOpt can identify optimal clas -
sification strategies tailored to different patterns in the data.
The hyperparameters of each classifier were also optimized to 
enhance generalization and mitigate overfitting. For LR, the regulari -
zation strength controls the trade-off between bias and variance by 
penalizing large coefficients. In KNN, the number of neighbors controls 
the bias-variance trade-off, with smaller values capturing fine-grained 
patterns, whereas larger values result in smoother decision bound -
aries. The choice of distance metric determines how similarity is 
measured, impacting the flexibility of the classifier in handling varying 
data geometries. For SVM, the type of kernel plays a critical role in 
shaping the decision boundary. Linear kernels provide simple, inter -
pretable boundaries, whereas the radial basis function (RBF) and sig -
moid kernels enable the model to learn nonlinear relationships in the 
data. RF hyperparameters, including the number of trees and minimum 
number of samples required to split a node, control the diversity and 
depth of the ensemble. A larger number of trees reduces variance but 
increases computational cost, whereas stricter split criteria reduce 
overfitting by limiting tree depth. For MLP, a single hidden layer ensures 
simplicity, whereas the number of neurons determines the capacity of 
the network to learn complex representations. The classifier models 
were implemented using the Scikit-learn Library [ 84 ].
Each stage of the EEG processing pipeline involves categorical 
choices, such as the selection of a denoising method, feature set, or 
classifier, followed by optimization of the respective hyperparameters. 
This results in a hierarchical search space, as shown in Fig. 4 , and 
summarized in Tables 1 and 2 .

scalability, particularly when dealing with complex, high-dimensional,

2.7.4. Quasi-Monte Carlo
QMC methods offer an efficient approach for sampling points from 
high-dimensional spaces to approximate integrals or optimize functions. 
Unlike traditional Monte Carlo methods, which rely on random sam -
pling, QMC methods use deterministic low-discrepancy sequences to 
generate points that are more uniformly distributed across the search 
space. The uniformity helps reduce gaps and clusters in the samples, 
leading to faster convergence in high-dimensional optimization prob -
lems. However, similar to CMA-ES, QMC is also not effective over con -
ditional or categorical distributions.
2.7.5. Random search
RS is a simple hyperparameter optimization method in which 
hyperparameter configurations are randomly selected from the search 
space without prior knowledge or assumptions about the function. RS 
does not build any surrogate function to model the hyperparameter 
space, and this randomness allows for broad exploration of the search 
space, which can sometimes lead to discovering high-performing con -
figurations. However, the efficiency of RS diminishes in high- 
dimensional spaces, as the probability of hitting the optimal or near- 
optimal region by chance becomes extremely low and often serves as 
a baseline performance indicator.
The different hyperparameter sampling methods or optimization 
algorithms were implemented using the Optuna Library [ 89 ]. To prevent 
information leakage, a new study was created for each optimization 
algorithm and dataset, ensuring that each study was independent and 
not influenced by hyperparameters evaluated in prior studies. For every 
study, 200 hyperparameter combinations, or ‘trials, ’ were assessed, with 
related information stored in a Structured Query Language (SQL) data -
base. Pruning was applied during optimization to enhance efficiency by 
discontinuing unpromising trials early. The classification scores across 
each cross-validation fold of the ongoing trials and for DL models, the 
classification performance across each epoch was compared with the 
median of previously completed trials. If the classification scores of the 
current trial were lower, then the trial was pruned. Pruning began only 
after a predefined number of trials had been conducted to ensure suffi -
cient progress before performance evaluation.
2.8. Metrics
The performance of each EEG processing pipeline was evaluated 
using a combination of cross-validation metrics during optimization and 
final test metrics after optimization. During optimization, in each trial 
the objective function was the MCC score, computed using 5-fold cross- 
validation on the training data. MCC was chosen due to its robustness in 
handling both imbalanced binary and multiclass datasets [ 90 – 92 ]. MCC 
evaluates the Pearson correlation between actual and predicted classi -
fications using a contingency matrix, minimizing the risk of overly 
optimistic results [ 92 ]. An MCC score of 1 signifies perfect agreement 
between actual and predicted values, whereas 0 indicates no correlation. 
In addition, ROC-AUC, Cohen ’ s kappa, weighted precision, recall, and 
F1-score were calculated for the optimized model on the test dataset to 
provide a holistic assessment of model performance. Accuracy is often 
skewed by class imbalances and tends to overestimate performance for 
the majority class [ 92 , 93 ]. Therefore, balanced accuracy was used as a 
more reliable alternative.
To assess computational costs, several timing metrics were recorded 
for each trial. These included the time spent selecting hyperparameters 
for preprocessing and classifiers, the time required for signal processing 
and feature extraction (signal processing time), and the time taken to 
perform 5-fold cross-validation for model evaluation (classification

2.9. Cache management system
The iterative search process in hyperparameter optimization requires 
repeated evaluations, which lead to redundant computations. To miti -

directory. If the cache key or cache file did not exist, the necessary

2.10. Deep learning-based EEG signal classification
The optimal EEG classification pipeline was compared with popular 
automated DL architectures, namely EEGNet, ShallowConvNet, and 
DeepConvNet [ 11 – 13 , 94 , 95 ].
EEGNet was designed for EEG signal analysis and is considered ideal 
for small datasets [ 12 , 96 ]. It consists of an initial 2D convolution layer 
that extracts feature maps representing EEG signals at various frequency 
bands. This is followed by a depthwise 2D convolution layer, which 
captures spatial information by learning spatial filters for each temporal 
filter inspired by the filter-bank common spatial pattern (FBCSP) algo -
rithm. Batch normalization is applied to stabilize learning, followed by 
an exponential linear unit activation function to introduce nonlinearity. 
A dropout layer is included to prevent overfitting. The model then uses 
separable convolutions, comprising depthwise and pointwise convolu -
tions, to efficiently capture spatiotemporal features. Key hyper -
parameters of the architecture include the number of temporal filters 
(F1), kernel length, the number of spatial filters per temporal filter (D), 
and dropout rate. The number of filters in the separable convolution 
layer (F2) is set to D × F1 [ 12 ].
ShallowConvNet focuses on spatial-temporal feature extraction 
analogous to traditional EEG processing, such as FBCSP, whereas 
DeepConvNet has a more complex structure with multiple convolution 
layers to capture EEG features across different scales [ 13 ]. The first 
block of ShallowConvNet consists of two layers: a temporal convolution 
layer and a spatial filtering layer. These layers are analogous to the 
bandpass filtering and spatial filtering steps in FBCSP. After temporal 
and spatial filtering, mean pooling and logarithmic activation are 
applied. DeepConvNet, by contrast, is a deeper ConvNet consisting of 
four convolutional-max-pooling blocks, with the first block similar to 
ShallowConvNet but with a smaller kernel size in the temporal convo -
lution. Following the first block, DeepConvNet includes three additional 
convolutional-max-pooling blocks with progressively more filters, 
enabling the model to learn hierarchical and abstract features from the 
EEG data. ELUs are used as the activation function throughout the 
network to improve gradient flow and learning stability [ 13 ]. The key 
hyperparameters of the architecture include the number of filters in each 
block, kernel size, and dropout rate.
The hyperparameters of the three DL models were also optimized 
using TPE sampling to achieve optimal classification performances. 
However, cross-validation was not applied during the hyperparameter 
optimization of DL models, as repeated model training was computa -
tionally expensive and impractical. Classification results of optimized 
DL models were compared with EEGOpt across different datasets.
3. Results
3.1. Optimization of EEG processing pipeline using EEGOpt
EEGOpt is an optimization framework for determining the optimal 
methodological choices and hyperparameter settings at each stage of an 
EEG signal processing and classification pipeline. At its core is a search 
algorithm that samples the hyperparameter space and determines the 
optimal configurations. We compared the performances of four sam -
pling algorithms: TPE, GP, CMA-ES, QMC, and RS. RS is considered a 
baseline.
The classification performances of the hyperparameters selected by 
each search process on the three datasets across three independent runs 
are listed in Table 3 . Using the TPE sampler, the highest MCC values 
achieved were 0.9513, 0.9918, and 0.9953, with maximum balanced 
accuracies of 96.42 %, 99.59 %, and 99.63 % for the ICM, NMED-E, and 
NMED-M datasets, respectively. The average balanced accuracies over 
the three runs were 92.78 %, 98.80 %, and 99.30 % for the ICM, NMED- 
E, and NMED-M datasets, respectively. TPE achieved very high classi -
fication performance and showed a significant improvement over RS, 
whereas the remaining samplers either underperformed or performed 
similarly to RS. The confusion matrices for the different samplers on the 
ICM dataset are shown in Fig. 6 .
Fig. 7 shows the optimization history across different samplers. Each 
dot represents a single trial with unique hyperparameters sampled from 
the search space, with corresponding objective values (classification 
performance measured using MCC) on the Y-axis. Initially, the hyper -
parameters were randomly sampled, as shown by the random distribu -
tion of objective values in the initial trials across all samplers, 
representing a broad exploration phase. The search process keeps track 
of the best-performing trials, as indicated by the blue line. For TPE, a 
greater number of trials converge progressively towards the best trial 
values, confirming that the search process identifies the global maxima 
and samples more densely around it. TPE also samples the hyper -
parameter space away from the optimal region to prevent the conver -
gence of the local maxima. In contrast, GP, which is also based on BO, 
failed to locate the global maxima and instead converged on a local 
maximum. Similarly, QMC and CMA-ES failed to converge on the global 
maxima, highlighting their inefficiency with categorical and tree- 
structured search spaces. Apart from TPE, the optimal configurations 
determined by the other samplers arise mostly by chance. These findings 
confirm that TPE is the optimal sampling method for traversing cate -
gorical and conditional search spaces, as in this study, and can be used to 
determine the optimal methodological choices for each dataset.
3.2. Optimal hyperparameter combinations
The EEGOpt framework offers a significant advantage over tradi -
tional DL methods by enabling the assessment of different hyper -
parameters and methodological choices for EEG classification. Fig. 8
shows the different combinations of methodological choices in the 
EEGOpt pipeline that led to the highest objective values for the three 
datasets in the form of a heatmap. For the ICM dataset, the combination 
of covariance features, WPD denoising, and the KNN classifier was 
optimal for achieving the highest classification performance. Notably, 
the combination of covariance features and the KNN classifier also 
performed well with EMD-based denoising and even in the absence of 
any denoising. However, EMD or no denoising adversely affected the 
classification performance when using fractal, wavelet, or entropy fea -
tures were used, where WPD remained the only optimal choice. KNN 
achieved the highest classification performance with all features except 
statistical features, which underperformed in all cases.
For the NMED-E and NMED-M datasets, the combination of WPD 
denoising and the KNN classifier continued to be the optimal choice for 
maximizing classification performance. The difference in classification 
performance between the denoising methods remained low, with WPD 
achieving the highest classification performance and EMD continuing to 
underperform with fractal and entropy features. LR, SVM, and statistical 
features consistently underperformed across all datasets. Compared 
with ICM, both covariance and wavelet features achieved high classifi -
cation performance. KNN achieved near-perfect classification across 
both datasets. MLP also performed better than the ICM dataset. These 
findings indicate that different feature sets and classifier combinations 
perform differently depending on the dataset.

4. Discussion
This study presents EEGOpt, a novel BO framework designed to 
automate, analyze, and optimize the different methodological choices 
involved in EEG signal processing and classification. Despite the rapid 
growth in EEG-based studies, identifying the optimal feature set, clas -
sifier, or preprocessing technique for a given dataset remains a major 
challenge. Several studies have identified different EEG signal process -
ing and classification options suited for specific applications; however, 
identifying a generalizable methodology suitable across multiple data -
sets and tasks is still lacking. Researchers agree that there is a need for 
methods that can determine the optimal approach to a particular 
problem [ 1 , 30 , 31 , 43 ]. However, Bergstra et al. noted that the question 
of how good a model is on a specific dataset is ill-posed; instead, the 
correct approach is to evaluate the quality of the best solution that a 
search procedure can discover for a particular application [ 28 ]. In our 
case, the pertinent question was how to design a framework that can 
search for the optimal configuration of an EEG signal classification 
pipeline while maintaining minimal computational requirements.
BO is a potent approach for solving such complex optimization 
problems. However, our approach differs from the conventional appli -
cation of BO for classifier tuning, as performed in several studies 
[ 57 – 60 ], where the focus was primarily on optimizing individual ML 
model hyperparameters. BO has also been used for feature selection 
[ 102 ], to optimize specific signal-processing hyperparameters, such as 
the number of filters or the selection of time windows in EEG signal 
processing [ 62 – 64 ], or selection of optimal classification algorithm 
[ 61 ]. However, to the best of our knowledge, BO has not been applied 
before to optimize each step of the EEG signal processing pipeline. A 
possible reason for the lack of such attempts may be due to the high 
computational costs associated with repeated signal transformations. 
Our study addressed this issue by introducing a modular caching 
mechanism. This caching mechanism led to over 70 % reduction on 
average in computation time compared to the non-cached baseline. This 
computational advantage was even more pronounced when compared 
to the time required for optimizing DL models such as EEGNet, Shal -
lowConvNet, and DeepConvNet. Moreover, EEGOpt consistently ach -
ieved a higher classification performance across all three datasets, 
outperforming popular DL architectures, making it a viable solution for 
determining optimal EEG processing and classification methods.
The proposed framework has several advantages. First, it simplifies 
the identification of signal processing and classification methods best 
suited to a dataset. Second, it facilitates the comparison of various signal 
processing and classification approaches. Third, it provides valuable 
insights into the intricate interdependencies among hyperparameters 
within the EEG processing and classification pipeline. EEGOpt also 
eliminates the need for a trial-and-error approach to identify optimal 
methodologies for EEG signal analysis and significantly reduces 
computational costs. Its modular design treats each pipeline step as a 
hyperparameter, enabling the seamless integration of diverse EEG 
signal-processing and classification techniques into the hyperparameter 
search space. This flexibility allows EEGOpt to be expanded by inte -
grating additional methods for signal processing, feature extraction, 
dimensionality reduction, and classification, thus making it a foundation 
for comparative evaluations across different datasets.
The application of EEGOpt to classify EEG signals under different 
music signals revealed that a combination of covariance or wavelet 
features and the KNN classifier effectively characterized the underlying 
neural dynamics. The consistent efficacy of covariance features aligns 
with the findings of Li et al. [ 103 ], suggesting the importance of func -
tional connectivity across brain regions under emotional stimuli. The 
KNN classifier has also been successful in several studies on emotion 
recognition and EEG classification [ 104 – 107 ]. There is also a preference 
for spectral and nonlinear features for emotion recognition, and SVM is

The results of this study also align with key neuroscientific findings

advancement, which eliminated the need for manual and qualitative

datasets. This adaptability of EEGOpt was further demonstrated using 
the NMED-E and NMED-M datasets, revealing how EEG processing 
configurations adapt to dataset-specific properties. For instance, while 
covariance features remained dominant for the ICM dataset, wavelet 
features were found to be optimal in the NMED-E and NMED-M datasets. 
Both studies reaffirm the core neuroscientific insight that music 
perception engages simultaneous linear, nonlinear, and spatial neural 
dynamics, with the relative contribution of each component varying 
across datasets and stimulus types.
At the core of the EEGOpt framework is an optimization algorithm 
that systematically explores the hyperparameter space to identify 
optimal configurations for a given dataset. Among the various optimi -
zation algorithms evaluated, TPE was the most suitable for EEG signal 
classification. This is because EEG pipelines often involve categorical 
choices, such as selecting feature extraction methods and classifiers, as 
well as hierarchical structures where certain hyperparameter settings 
depend on previous selections. Similar to TPE, GP are compatible with 
categorical and conditional hyperparameters; however, they often face 
computational challenges when applied to high-dimensional search 
spaces. Algorithms designed for continuous optimization problems tend 
to underperform in these contexts. For instance, CMA-ES, an evolu -
tionary algorithm, and QMC, which uses deterministic sampling, 
showed lower performance in our experiments. Although Genetic Al -
gorithms (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) are widely used 
in EEG research, they are not inherently suited to tree-structured or 
conditional search spaces. GA are best suited for combinatorial prob -
lems, whereas PSO excels in continuous optimization problems. 
Adapting these algorithms would require non-trivial modifications to 
encoding schemes and search logic and yet may not offer significant 
advantages over TPE [ 110 , 111 ]. Moreover, population-based methods 
like GA incur higher computational costs due to evaluation of multiple 
candidate configurations per generation, which limits cache reuse and 
increases runtime. Therefore, standard GA/PSO implementations were 
excluded from our comparative analysis; however, future work could 
consider their adapted forms for a broader comparison of optimization 
strategies.
In recent years, DL architectures have gained preference for EEG 
signal classification [ 109 ]. Although DL models are considered suitable 
for automated EEG signal classification, they provide little information 
regarding the underlying processes and have high implementation and 
computational costs [ 11 ]. Lotte et al. suggested that ML models should 
be preferred over DL for applications with low data availability and 
portability [ 3 ]. In contrast, EEGOpt provides insight into hyper -
parameter interactions and supports implementation of lightweight ML 
models tailored to specific use cases. DL models also face certain limi -
tations based on their architectures. For example, the EEGNet frame -
work mimics the FBCSP algorithm, whereas DeepConvNet uses multiple 
convolutional layers for multiscale analysis. In contrast, EEGOpt can 
implement a wider range of features depending on the feature extraction 
methods, including covariance, wavelet, and fractal analyses, auto -
matically selecting the method best suited for a dataset.
Despite its promising performance, EEGOpt has certain limitations 
that warrant discussion. The performance of EEGOpt depends on the 
selection of feature extraction and classification methods. It is necessary 
to consider the computational performance of each component in the 
pipeline, as they may become bottlenecks in the search process. For 
instance, feature sets such as sample entropy or classifiers such as MLP 
or SVM can be computationally intensive, potentially hindering the 
optimization process. To mitigate this, avoiding computationally 
intensive feature sets and utilizing performance-efficient implementa -
tions of MLP or SVM, such as those employing stochastic gradient 
descent, or reducing the number of cross-validation folds can improve 
computational performance. Large EEG datasets with high dimension -
ality can also increase computational expenses, which can be alleviated 
by implementing dimensionality reduction early in the pipeline. More -
over, the efficacy of the caching mechanism depends on the availability 
of sufficient storage space for cached signal transformations. On large 
datasets, a restricted cache size may lead to frequent deletion and 
regeneration of cache files, reducing the computational benefits of 
caching. While EEGOpt uses TPE effectively to navigate hierarchical 
spaces, TPE may converge to local optima in highly noisy or complex 
search spaces. Visual inspection of the optimization history and hyper -
parameter distributions can help detect such issues.
The DL models were implemented using the same hardware as 
EEGOpt to ensure a fair comparison. However, the computation times 
for both the EEGOpt and DL models can be significantly improved by 
implementing deeper parallelization and utilizing GPU architectures, 
although this may involve higher costs. For performance evaluation,

randomly split into training and testing sets. This is a common approach 
in EEG optimization studies, as it enables benchmarking of EEGOpt 
against other models on a shared dataset [ 19 ]. Although this strategy 
provided a comprehensive assessment of overall classification perfor -
mance, it did not explicitly evaluate generalization to unseen in -
dividuals. Performing such analyses would require re-optimizing the 
entire pipeline separately for each participant. Additionally, statistical 
significance testing was not included due to the limited number of 
test-set observations. As noted by Roy et al. [ 19 ], statistical testing is 
often absent or improperly applied in comparable EEG studies with 
constrained evaluation trials. Robust statistical comparisons, as recom -
mended by Dem ˇsar [ 112 ] and by Japkowicz and Shah [ 113 ], require 
repeated cross-validation or resampling-based evaluation across a large 
number of test-set observations. While these limitations do not affect the 
core contribution of this study, future work should incorporate statisti -
cally rigorous evaluations and subject-wise generalization to further 
validate EEGOpt ’ s performance across diverse domains and against a 
broader range of deep learning models.
The core contribution of EEGOpt lies in its ability to systematically 
identify optimal EEG processing strategies, making it a promising 
framework for real-world applications where neural dynamics are 
complex and optimal analysis strategies remain unclear. As the efficacy 
of an EEG classification pipeline depends on multiple interacting factors 
[ 30 – 32 ], determining whether a specific configuration is truly optimal is 
a significant challenge [ 43 ]. EEGOpt addresses this issue, making it 
highly valuable across diverse EEG-based domains, including 
brain-computer interface (BCI) paradigms (e.g., motor imagery, emotion 
recognition), neuroscience research, and clinical diagnostics. For 
instance, in neurological conditions such as epilepsy [ 114 ], Alzheimer ’ s 
disease [ 115 ], frontotemporal dementia [ 116 ], or Kleefstra syndrome 
[ 117 ], the underlying EEG markers are often uncertain. In motor reha -
bilitation following stroke or injury, EEG patterns related to motor 
intention differ across individuals and recovery stages [ 118 ]. Similarly, 
in emotion recognition and music therapy [ 119 , 120 ], EEG responses 
vary with emotional state, context, and stimuli. In such cases, EEGOpt 
may enable data-driven discovery of effective feature extraction and 
classification methods by identifying dataset-specific neural dynamics 
and EEG response patterns. Moreover, its modular design allows seam -
less integration of new methods into the search space, further enhancing 
real-world applicability. This may enable researchers to evaluate the 
efficacy of novel signal processing or classification techniques against 
established baselines, as well as to assess how these methods may in -
fluence each stage of the signal processing and classification pipeline.
EEGOpt also has potential real-time applications. Although the full 
optimization cycle is not suitable for real-time use due to its iterative 
nature, the optimized pipelines obtained through the search process can 
be deployed independently with minimal computational overhead. 
EEGOpt may also be extended to support multi-objective optimization, 
aiming to minimize computation time while maximizing classification 
performance. Classification approaches such as FBCSP and Riemannian 
geometry-based methods have demonstrated computationally efficient 
performance suitable for real-time applications [ 121 ]. Future studies 
may integrate these methods in EEGOpt and compare their computa -
tional performance across datasets. Future studies should aim to 
empirically validate the efficacy of EEGOpt in diverse EEG-based do -
mains and real-world applications.
5. Conclusion
EEGOpt is a scalable, performance-efficient framework for auto -
mated EEG signal classification that addresses the long-standing chal -
lenge that no single methodology generalizes across EEG applications. It 
systematically explores and evaluates different signal processing and 
classification methods to identify optimal configurations. By automating 
this selection process, EEGOpt eliminates the need for manual trial-and- 
error in EEG signal analysis.
EEGOpt also offers insights into the interdependencies among pre -
processing, feature extraction, and classification steps; relationships that 
are often opaque in black-box models. This interpretability makes it a 
valuable foundation for comparative evaluations and methodological 
refinement. Although this study focused on music-based EEG datasets, 
the framework is generalizable and can be extended to other domains by 
incorporating task-specific methodological options into the search 
space.
A system capable of identifying optimal EEG processing strategies 
while maintaining classification performance and computational effi -
ciency is relevant across a wide range of EEG research and application 
domains. EEGOpt may be particularly useful in neuroscience and 
healthcare contexts, where it has the potential to improve the efficiency 
and reliability of EEG-based diagnostic and monitoring systems. As 
neuroscientific research continues to evolve, the insights gained from 
this study can support the ongoing development of optimized, inter -
pretable EEG analysis methodologies.
